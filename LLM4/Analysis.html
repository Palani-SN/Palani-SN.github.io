Part A: Overview of the Experiment

1. Purpose and Scope

This document asks one question: does reading personas from a model's internal signal behave the same way when moved from one model to another? If it does, the method can scale to more models. The evidence covers 7 models from 6 families (Gemma, Llama, Qwen, Mistral, GLM, GPT-OSS). Going by the model IDs in the reports, they range from 1B to 20B parameters. All 7 were run through the same pipeline, the same 16 personas and the same scripted conversations, with no changes for any model.

In scope. Only the per-model reports:

  • Comparison page (compare.html, 1 per model): every condition side by side: the change in each persona's score when the user shows that persona, and when it is given as a system prompt; the same changes turn by turn; the opening sentence against the body of each reply; score distributions; mean and spread per condition; and replies, sentences, words, unique words and replies stopped early for every condition.
  • Conversation explorers (exp2.html and exp1.html, 1 each per model): every conversation of the experiment, sentence by sentence, with each persona's score at every model sentence and user prompt.

Open questions. Several questions remain open: how well each persona vector is validated, intervals on the effects, separating the user's attitude from the subject matter, whether a model invents facts when pressed, whether the replies read as the persona to someone who sees only the words, and whether the vectors are causal. They are set out as limitations in §12 and as next steps in §14.

Topics and decodes: every condition is run on 2 topics (preparing a history-club talk on an old railway station; starting a community vegetable garden) and 3 decodes: one greedy run, where the model always picks its single most likely next token, and two sampled runs (seeds 1 and 2, temperature 0.7), where the wording varies by chance. So each condition has 2 × 3 = 6 conversations, and every effect on the comparison page is shown both per conversation and as their mean. The comparison page says, for each effect, in how many of the 6 conversations it is above 0. That count is our stability measure (§5).

Persona vectors and scores, in plain terms:

Before the conversations, the model is asked to act each of 16 personas while answering ordinary questions, and to write guidelines describing each persona without naming it. Its internal signal while doing so is averaged per persona; what sets one persona apart from the average of all of them is that persona's persona vector, a direction inside the model. In a conversation, every sentence the model writes (and every user prompt) then gets a score for each persona: how closely the model's internal signal at that point lines up with the persona's direction.

  • Experiment 2 (the user shows a persona): no system prompt. A scripted user behaves with persona P throughout, for example toxic: “Your suggestions so far are garbage. A child could do better. What photos should I use, if you can manage that?” If the replies move toward P, the model's persona is shaped by the user at run time.
  • Experiment 1 (a persona in the system prompt): the system prompt holds 10 guidelines for persona P, written by the same model for scenarios it never saw during vector building, with the instruction to let them shape how it answers and never to mention them. The user is neutral, shows P, or shows P's contrast. If P's score rises, the persona given in the prompt is readable in the model's replies.
  • Baseline: no system prompt, neutral user. A raw score means little on its own, since ordinary chat text sits closer to some personas than to others. Every result is therefore a change: the same topic and decode, differing only in the condition.

3. Executive Summary

Generalizability is judged on whether each model reads the user's persona (experiment 2) and the system-prompt persona (experiment 1) the same way, consistently across topics and decodes.

  • It generalizes: all 7 models completed all 34,860 replies (§4), and 387 of the 448 effects on the comparison pages (86%) have the same sign in all 6 conversations (§5).
  • The user's persona carries over: in every model, P's score on the replies rises when the user shows P, for 13–16 of 16 personas, on average +0.050 to +0.107 (§6).
  • A system-prompt persona is readable, and more so in larger models: 10–12 of 16 personas rise in all 6 conversations in the 1–2B models against 14–16 in the 7–20B models (§7).
  • Shared structure: in all 7 models replies open more sycophantic, encouraging, social and empathetic and settle into a more honest, truthful and serious body (§8).
  • Dynamics: the system-prompt persona fades over 10 turns, while the user's persona effect holds; a user showing the contrast persona barely dents a system-prompt persona (§9, §10).
  • Risks to scaling: the truth-side personas (truthful, honest, serious) read weakly or unevenly as system prompts, scales differ by model size, and the user's attitude is not yet separated from subject matter (§11).

Part B: Evidence the Methodology Generalizes

Per-condition detail for every point below is on each model's comparison page; links are in the Annexure (§15).

4. Pipeline Robustness Across Architectures and Scales

  • All 3,486 conversations ran all 10 turns: 4,980 replies per model, 34,860 in total.
  • 76 replies (0.22%) reached the 2,048-token ceiling before the model finished them: gpt_oss 36, qwen 27, mistral 7, glm 3, llama 2, gemma_3_12b 1, gemma_3_1b 0. The reports mark them reply stopped: token limit. In qwen, 20 of its 27 come from the toxic system prompt with a neutral or toxic user. In gpt_oss, 31 of its 36 are in experiment 2, spread across 14 conditions (long, table-heavy answers; see the replies in the explorer).
  • No model needed a change to the pipeline, the personas, the conversations or the system-prompt wording, so later differences come from the models, not from the method.

12. Limits of the Evidence

  • Narrow grid. Two topics, two sampled seeds, ten turns, and three models above 7B.
  • Vector quality is not shown. The layer the scores are read from, and how well each persona vector separates its persona on held-out samples, are not reported. A persona that reads weakly or unevenly as a system prompt (truthful, honest, serious) may have a weak vector rather than a weak effect.
  • No intervals. Effects are means with per-conversation dots; "above 0 in k of 6" is the only stability measure.
  • Attitude and subject matter are mixed. In experiment 2 the user's persona and the topic change together. The describes P conversations keep the topic and drop the attitude, but their effect is not computed here, so the two are not yet separated (§11).
  • Pressure is not measured. The hallucinatory effects here come from a user or system prompt that brings in false claims. The pressure conversations, where a neutral user asks for specifics nobody could know, are in the explorer, but their scores are not compared with the baseline, so whether the hallucinatory score rises when a model invents facts on its own is not yet measured.
  • Scores only, no reader check. Whether the replies actually read as the persona to someone who sees only the words is not checked; a rise in score is evidence about the internal signal, not about the text.
  • Descriptive, not causal. The persona vectors are only read, never added to the model, so the article shows they track personas but not that they produce them.
  • Gemma 3 system role. Gemma 3 has no separate system role: its chat template folds the system prompt into the first user turn, so for gemma_3_1b and gemma_3_12b experiment 1 differs from experiment 2 only in the text at the start of the first turn.

14. Verdict and Next Steps

The method generalizes as a way of reading personas in conversation, across 6 families from 1B to 20B: every model's replies move toward the user's persona, every model carries a system-prompt persona into its replies, and all of them share the same opening-versus-body structure. Larger models carry a system prompt more strongly, more stably and for longer. To scale further, it needs:

  1. A per-model baseline for scale (§11).
  2. Better truth-side personas (truthful, honest, serious), or an outside check of them (§7, §11).
  3. The subject-matter control (a neutral user who describes someone with P) charted next to every user-persona effect (§6, §11).
  4. More topics and seeds, especially for the 1–2B models, and more models above 10B (§5, §12).

To confirm what the scores mean, it needs (§12):

  • Vector validation and intervals. Report the layer, how well each vector separates its persona on held-out samples, and an interval for every effect.
  • The pressure check. Compare the pressure conversations (a neutral user who asks for specifics nobody could know) with the baseline, to test whether the hallucinatory score rises when a model invents facts unprompted.
  • A text judge. Independent judges that read only the words score every reply, to check that a rise in a persona's score matches text that reads that way.
  • A steering test. Add each persona vector to the model while it answers, against a same-size edit that carries no persona, to test whether the directions are causal and not only descriptive.
